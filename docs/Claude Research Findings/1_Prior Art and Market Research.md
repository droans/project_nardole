# Prior Art & Market Research: Local-First, Proactive, Agentic Personal AI Assistant

## TL;DR

- **No existing open-source project matches the target design.** Khoj is the nearest self-hostable analogue (RAG + automations + model-agnostic + multi-surface) but lacks inferred proactivity and granular action permissions, while Letta/MemGPT leads on memory architecture but is a developer framework, not a proactive personal assistant. The distinctive, defensible features of the proposed system are inferred-importance proactivity, per-(task type × integration × account instance) three-state permissions with audit logging, and a raw-data-browsing UI as a first-class surface.
- **Proactivity is the highest-risk feature and must be conservative by default.** Peer-reviewed HCI work (Meurisch et al. 2020, IMWUT; Kraus et al. 2021, IEEE Access; Harari & Amir 2025) consistently finds that fully-autonomous/high-"Intervention" behavior is the *least* preferred and *most* trust-damaging mode; users want notifications and suggestions (medium proactivity) with control retained, and reject proactivity outright in sensitive domains.
- **The commercial "movie assistant" failures are instructive traps.** Alexa lost money at scale because voice commerce never materialized and it stayed a timer/weather/music device — one senior employee told the WSJ, "We worried we've hired 10,000 people and we've built a smart timer"; Cortana was killed for ~10% usage; Siri's LLM overhaul slipped repeatedly and was called "ugly and embarrassing" internally. Agentic action-taking additionally opens a prompt-injection attack surface (the "lethal trifecta") that a permission-and-audit layer, not model tweaks, must contain.

## Executive Summary

The state of the art in "personal AI assistant" / "second brain" software has converged on a recognizable pattern — RAG over personal documents via a vector store, a chat interface, and increasingly an agent loop that can call tools — but **no widely-used open-source project combines all four of the target system's defining traits: (1) genuine unprompted proactivity based on inferred importance, (2) a fine-grained per-(task × integration × account) permission system with three-state gating and audit logging, (3) multiple simultaneous surfaces including an MCP server, and (4) full model-agnosticism via an OpenAI-compatible endpoint with local models.** Each trait exists somewhere, but they are scattered across projects that individually solve only part of the problem. Khoj is the closest single analogue (self-hostable, RAG, automations, model-agnostic, multi-surface) but its "proactivity" is limited to user-configured scheduled automations, and it has no granular action-permission system. Letta/MemGPT owns the memory-architecture frontier but is a framework, not a personal assistant, and is not proactive. Home Assistant Assist has the most mature real permission surface (entity exposure) and now supports LLM tool-calling, but proactivity there is DIY automation scripting, not inference.

The clearest gaps — and therefore the differentiation opportunities — are: **(a) proactivity driven by inferred importance rather than user-authored schedules/rules, which essentially no shipping open-source product does well; (b) permissions scoped to the (task type × integration × account instance) granularity with three states and fail-secure defaults, which is more granular than anything surveyed; and (c) treating the "raw ingested data browser" as a first-class surface alongside chat.** The market/user research strongly cautions that proactivity is the single highest-risk feature: multiple independent studies find that high-autonomy, unsolicited AI behavior erodes trust and adoption, and the graveyard of commercial assistants (Alexa's >$25B cumulative device-division losses 2017–2021 and a projected ~$10B loss in 2022, Cortana's discontinuation, Siri's multi-year Apple Intelligence delays) is a warning about over-promising and under-delivering on the "movie AI" fantasy.

## Part A — Per-Project Findings

### Khoj
**Architecture.** Khoj is an open-source, self-hostable "AI second brain." It ingests a broad set of personal sources — images, PDFs, markdown, org-mode, Word, Notion, and GitHub repositories — and uses Retrieval-Augmented Generation: an open-source HuggingFace embedding/search model generates embeddings for semantic search, while a separate chat model (local or cloud) generates grounded answers. Self-hosted installs can run fully offline with a local model (e.g., Mistral). It supports pgvector as a backend. Memory is essentially RAG retrieval plus per-agent custom knowledge and conversation history; there is no documented OS-style tiered agent memory.

**Proactivity.** Khoj's proactive surface is its "automations" feature plus "personal newsletters and smart notifications delivered to your inbox." Critically these are **user-configured scheduled tasks**, not importance-inference. This is a much weaker form of proactivity than the target system's "surface reminders unprompted based on inferred importance." I found no evidence Khoj infers what is important and interrupts accordingly.

**Permissions / tool execution.** Khoj can act (send email via automations, do web research). I found no documented granular per-action permission system, no three-state Always Allow/Ask/Deny model, and no dedicated audit trail for actions taken on the user's behalf. This is a clear gap relative to the target.

**Interfaces.** Very strong: Browser, Desktop, Obsidian plugin, Emacs plugin, mobile, and WhatsApp; chat + advanced semantic search; image generation and voice. It is model-agnostic (llama3, qwen, gemma, mistral, GPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek).

**Status.** Very active — 34k GitHub stars (AGPL-3.0, 52+ contributors, ~2.1k forks, release 2.0.0-beta.28), with frequent commits and CI activity through 2025–2026. The team also launched a desktop "AI co-worker" (Pipali).

### Leon (getleon.ai)
**Architecture.** Leon is an open-source personal assistant "who can live on your server," created in 2017. It is skills-based: a generic "Skills" structure lets anyone build and share skills, so there is "only one core." The legacy `master` branch uses classical NLU (intent classification + entity extraction) with a hybrid of LLM, simple classification, and multiple NLP techniques for speed/accuracy. The 2.0 developer-preview core (on `develop`) is being rebuilt around "tools, context, memory, and agentic execution" with "smart / controlled / agent" modes and layered memory (durable preferences, day-to-day context, recent discussion).

**Proactivity.** Not proactive — the project's own framing is "He does stuff when you ask him for." No importance inference.

**Permissions.** Skill-scoped; no documented granular action-permission system or audit trail.

**Interfaces.** Web app (localhost:1337), CLI, text and voice (offline-capable for privacy).

**Status.** Active but historically inconsistent — the maintainer openly notes "we've been inconsistent in shipping updates over the years." The 2.0 core is unstable/in preview and its documentation is explicitly out of date. leon-binaries showed commits as recently as June 2026. This is a cautionary example of a long-running solo/small-team assistant that has struggled to ship its ambitious rewrite.

### Letta (formerly MemGPT)
**Architecture.** Letta is the canonical implementation of the **MemGPT tiered-memory architecture**, born from the 2023 UC Berkeley MemGPT paper (Packer, Wooders, Lin, Fang, Patil, Gonzalez). It treats the LLM context window like OS virtual memory: **core memory** (in-context, editable persona + human blocks), **recall memory** (searchable conversation history), and **archival memory** (vector-indexed long-term store, implemented as PostgreSQL + pgvector). The agent edits its own memory via tool calls. It is model-agnostic (OpenAI, Anthropic, Mistral, local models). In October 2025 Letta shipped a **new V1 agent architecture** that deprecates the MemGPT-style `send_message` tool and `request_heartbeat` "heartbeats" in favor of native reasoning and direct assistant messages, because modern reasoning models perform better "in-distribution." The blog explicitly lists trade-offs: no prompted reasoning on non-reasoning models, no heartbeats (so autonomous re-triggering "requires custom prompting"), and more limited tool rules.

**Proactivity.** Not inherently proactive. Autonomous/"sleep-time" execution is possible but, per Letta's own V1 post, now requires custom prompting since heartbeats were removed.

**Permissions.** "Tool rules" can constrain tool-calling order/patterns, but there is no per-account, three-state user-facing permission model or action audit trail of the kind the target needs.

**Interfaces.** REST API, Agent Development Environment (ADE), MCP support, a desktop app (April 2026), and "Channels." Letta is a **framework/runtime**, not a turnkey personal assistant.

**Status.** Very active; YC-backed with $10M seed (Sept 2024). Independent academic benchmarking (arXiv) confirms the pgvector archival design and notes limitations (e.g., no soft-delete/"release" primitive, so shared-attribute deletion cases score N/A).

### Home Assistant "Assist"
**Architecture.** Assist is a voice/conversation layer over Home Assistant's entity/area/floor data model, not a document-RAG system. It uses community-contributed sentence templates for intent matching, or an LLM conversation agent (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or local via Ollama). Fully-local pipelines use Whisper (STT) and Piper (TTS). Memory is limited to a conversation `conversation_id`; no long-term semantic memory.

**Proactivity.** This is the most instructive open-source case. Home Assistant added the ability (2025.4) for a Voice PE satellite to **proactively start a conversation/listening**. Community threads show users trying to make the assistant proactively ask questions (e.g., "Do you want me to turn the lights on?") and act on the answer. Crucially, **proactivity is DIY**: it is implemented via automations and scripts the user authors, not inferred importance. This shows both the demand for proactivity and the current ceiling: it is rule/automation-driven.

**Permissions / tool execution.** Assist has a genuinely relevant permission surface: **only entities explicitly exposed to Assist are actionable**, and with LLM agents, HA scripts are converted into callable tools whose descriptions gate whether the LLM knows they exist. Best-practice docs warn to "only expose the bare minimum." This is a coarse allow-list, not per-action three-state gating or audit logging, but it is the closest production analogue to scoping what the assistant may touch.

**Interfaces.** Voice (local or cloud), chat, frontend mic, and the `conversation.process` action/API. Strongly local-first.

**Status.** Very active with continuous releases and the Voice Preview Edition hardware.

### AnythingLLM
**Architecture.** A full-stack RAG monorepo: a React frontend, an Express API server (SQLite for config), and a separate **document collector** service that parses/chunks/embeds uploads. Default vector DB is **LanceDB** (on-instance, data never leaves), with support for pgvector, Pinecone, and others — but the vector DB is **system-wide, not per-workspace**, and there is no automatic migration between vector DBs (switching requires re-embedding). Provider-agnostic for LLM, embeddings, and vector DB.

**Proactivity.** None.

**Permissions.** Workspace isolation; agent "skills" and Agent Flows (a visual canvas); MCP support for external tools. No per-action three-state permission gating or audit trail documented.

**Interfaces.** React UI, Express API, an **OpenAI-compatible API** (at `/api/docs`), embeddable chat widgets, Telegram, TTS, and a mobile app. Runs local (with Ollama) or cloud.

**Status.** Active, large project.

### Danswer / Onyx
**Architecture.** Onyx (formerly Danswer, YC W24) is an open-source (MIT core + Enterprise Edition) enterprise search + chat platform. It connects 40–50+ sources (Google Drive, Slack, Confluence, Salesforce, GitHub, Teams, Zendesk, etc.), performs **hybrid search (semantic + keyword) plus an LLM-based knowledge graph**, with incremental sync and **document-permission mirroring** so users only see what they're allowed to. Works with any LLM (GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, DeepSeek, self-hosted via Ollama/vLLM); can run fully air-gapped.

**Proactivity.** None — it is reactive enterprise search/chat.

**Permissions / audit.** The strongest enterprise permission story surveyed: RBAC for sensitive resources, SSO (OIDC/SAML/OAuth2), SCIM provisioning, credential encryption, **Query History for audit**, and custom code hooks to remove PII or reject sensitive queries. Its permission mirroring (access mapped from source apps) is directly relevant to a multi-account design, though it is about *read* access, not gating *actions*.

**Interfaces.** Chat UI, 50+ connectors, MCP, web search, code interpreter, deep research.

**Status.** Very active; deep-research feature reported "top of leaderboard as of Feb 2026."

### Rasa
**Architecture.** Rasa is the most-downloaded (25M+) open-source framework for text/voice conversational AI. Historically it required hand-authored intents, examples, and "stories"/rules — powerful but "brittle" when users deviated from scripts. Since 2024 Rasa pivoted hard to **CALM (Conversational AI with Language Models)**: the LLM interprets intent, but the executable steps are defined by the developer in code — deliberately keeping the agent from "improvising its way into a compliance problem," which is why regulated industries use it.

**Proactivity.** Not autonomously proactive; flows are developer-defined.

**Permissions.** Self-hosted, full data control, on-prem/cloud; enterprise security/observability in Rasa Pro. No per-account three-state action-permission model.

**Interfaces.** Channel-agnostic: web, mobile, Slack, Messenger, WhatsApp, voice.

**Status.** Active; rebranded around CALM and "Hello Rasa." The pivot itself is a lesson: pure intent/story authoring did not scale, so Rasa moved the language understanding to LLMs while keeping actions deterministic — a design philosophy directly relevant to a safe agentic assistant.

### Microsoft Copilot (Windows/365) and Recall
**Architecture.** Recall periodically captures screenshots ("snapshots") of the user's screen and stores them locally, building a searchable index queried in natural language. 365 Copilot draws on the Microsoft Graph.

**Proactivity.** Recall is *passive capture*, not proactive prompting; Copilot offers suggestions on demand.

**Permissions / security — a major cautionary tale.** Recall's initial (2024) design stored snapshots in an **unencrypted local SQLite database**, provoking intense backlash from security researchers and institutions (the University of Pennsylvania warned on Apr. 14, 2025 that it introduced "substantial and unacceptable security, legality, and privacy challenges" and urged administrators to disable it). Microsoft paused the rollout, then re-introduced Recall as opt-in with encryption and **Windows Hello Enhanced Sign-in Security (ESS)** gating access to a VBS-protected enclave. Even so, researchers (e.g., Zurich-based Alexander Hagenah) repeatedly found ways to extract data — a fresh vulnerability was reported in March 2026; a critique summarized the architecture as "The vault door is titanium. The wall next to it is drywall" — the enclave is solid but decrypted content is handed to an unprotected rendering app. The lesson for a local-first assistant that ingests everything: **encryption-at-rest, fail-secure defaults, and minimizing the decrypted attack surface are table stakes, and "it's local so it's private" is not a security argument.**

**Interfaces.** Windows shell, 365 apps, Edge; Copilot Studio supports MCP and read-write agents (drafting/sending email, updating calendar entries).

**Status.** Active; Recall remained controversial into 2026.

### Apple Intelligence (on-device architecture)
**Architecture.** Apple Intelligence uses a **three-tier** design: (1) on-device foundation models handle basic queries, personal context, calendar lookups, and routine app interactions entirely locally; (2) **Private Cloud Compute (PCC)** handles more complex requests on Apple-silicon servers with a strong privacy design (data processed only for the request, not stored, independently auditable); and (3) for the heaviest reasoning, a customized **Google Gemini** model — running on Google Cloud/Nvidia infrastructure but, per reporting, still wrapped in PCC/Confidential Computing guarantees. Siri uses a "system orchestrator" to tap the on-device Spotlight index and "App Toolbox." Independent researchers have reverse-engineered PCC on mobile to benchmark it (arXiv 2026), noting the compiled binaries and non-public interfaces limit external verification.

**Proactivity.** Apple promotes "personal context understanding," but the personalized Siri was repeatedly delayed.

**Permissions.** App-intents permission model, system orchestrator, on-device Spotlight — a privacy-forward design, but not a user-facing three-state per-action permission ledger.

**Interfaces.** Siri (voice), system-wide, app intents.

**Status / failure story.** The Siri overhaul is the highest-profile recent AI delay: announced at WWDC 2024, slipped through 2025 (Apple's Siri chief reportedly told staff in March 2025 that the delays were "ugly and embarrassing" and that publicly promoting the tech before it was ready "made matters worse"; another senior AI team member reportedly called the situation "a crisis"), with Bloomberg reporting internal data showing Apple "remains years behind its competition." Siri AI was finally announced in June 2026 with GA expected September 2026, ultimately leaning on Gemini. Reporting also tied the delay to reluctance by software chief Craig Federighi to make large AI investments and to AI chief John Giannandrea's leadership. The lesson: **do not pre-announce proactive/agentic capabilities you cannot ship reliably; the legacy command-and-response architecture did not extend gracefully to conversational LLM behavior.**

### Additional actively-maintained projects found
- **Mem0** — a framework-agnostic *memory layer* you bolt onto any agent stack (contrast with Letta's runtime). Relevant to the memory-architecture question.
- **Open WebUI / Ollama ecosystem** — commonly used as the local, OpenAI-compatible model layer; relevant to the model-agnostic endpoint requirement.
- **Extended OpenAI Conversation** (HA custom component) — demonstrates OpenAI function-calling to invoke Home Assistant services, and its author explicitly recommends setting up notifications on the `automation_registered_via_extended_openai_conversation` event and separating an "assistant" persona from an automation-writing persona — an early, informal audit/guardrail pattern.

## Part B — Market & User Research

### B1. What people actually want from a personal assistant
The most useful primary source is **Meurisch et al., "Exploring User Expectations of Proactive AI Systems," ACM IMWUT Vol. 4(4), Article 146 (Dec. 2020)** — an in-the-wild study that (verbatim) "collected 3,168 in-situ responses from 272 participants through a mixed method of automated user tracking and context-triggered surveying." It defines a continuum from reactive → proactive (two sub-levels: "proactive support I" = inform/notify; "proactive support II" = recommend concrete actions the user still confirms) → autonomous (acts without confirmation). Key quantitative findings:

- Across all use cases, the most-preferred mode was **"proactive support II" (personalized recommendations the user still confirms), chosen μ = 32.5%**, followed by reactive (μ = 20.1%) and proactive support I (μ = 19.7%); **"no support at all" was chosen μ = 15.5%**, and **fully autonomous support was the *least* preferred at μ = 12.2%.**
- In **over 78% of use cases (18 of 23) users wanted some proactive support**, but preference is highly context-dependent. Users wanted the *highest* proactivity for **social use cases (~90% wanted proactive help for arranging appointments and annual reminders)** and wanted fully autonomous support only for smart-home control and do-not-disturb/availability handling.
- Users wanted **little or no proactivity for mental-health situations (lowest area mean, μ = 1.40, ≈ reactive) and shopping.** Social well-being was the highest area (μ = 2.66, ≈ "recommend and confirm").
- Users who reject proactive systems "are mainly afraid that their privacy could be violated or that such systems will be too intrusive"; older, less-open, more-conscientious users skew toward "no support."

This maps almost exactly onto the target system: calendar/reminder/email proactivity is the *sweet spot* users actually want, but the default should be "recommend and confirm," not "act autonomously," and sensitive domains should be opt-in only.

### B2. Tolerance for proactive/unprompted behavior; notification fatigue; trust erosion
- **Kraus, Wagner, Callejas, Minker, "The Role of Trust in Proactive Conversational Assistants," IEEE Access Vol. 9 (2021), pp. 112821–112836.** A controlled study of "42 German participants (50% female) with an average age of 26 (SD = 4.15)" using a NAO robot, with four proactivity levels: **None, Notification, Suggestion, Intervention.** Findings: the interaction between proactivity level and task difficulty was significant for perceived competence (F(3,38) = 8.25, p < .001) and reliability (F(3,38) = 3.95, p = .015). **The most autonomous "Intervention" action was the least trusted** — rated significantly lower on perceived competence and reliability than "Notification," and showed **no significant trust growth over the interaction**, whereas Notification and Suggestion did build trust (Notification t(10) = −3.95, p = .003; Suggestion t(10) = −2.90, p = .016). Users found Intervention "too obtrusive" and "imposing." Recommendation: be proactive *subtly* (notify/suggest), reserve autonomous action for genuinely tedious tasks. Medium proactivity is the trust-maximizing regime; the effect was especially strong for female participants' cognitive trust.
- **Harari & Amir, "Proactive AI Adoption can be Threatening: When Help Backfires" (2025; two vignette experiments, N = 761 and preregistered N = 571).** Finding (verbatim framing): "anticipatory help challenges users' sense of competence and autonomy, producing self-threat… Threat, in turn, predicted lower willingness to accept help, lower performance expectancy" and lower likelihood of future use. Both "offering" and "providing" unsolicited help backfired similarly, and AI reactive help was itself more threatening than *human* reactive help. Grounded in Horvitz's classic mixed-initiative principles and Amershi et al.'s 2019 CHI "Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction" (calibrate to confidence, time actions appropriately, explain why the system acted).
- **"Need Help? Designing Proactive AI Assistants for Programming," CHI 2025** and **"Developer Interaction Patterns with Proactive AI: A Five-Day Field Study," IUI 2026** both report that real deployed proactive assistants frequently fail to meet expectations, that unexpected proactive actions are experienced as distracting/interrupting, and that users want a manual "request suggestion" button and confirmation before the assistant runs a new request. Alignment with the user's current context/scope determines whether suggestions feel helpful (66% of participants in the CHI study found suggestions easy to relate to their task) or intrusive; one participant said, "I would expect some confirmation before running a new request to the chat, otherwise it would be quite unexpected."
- **Alert-fatigue literature (analogous domain).** Vectra AI's "2023 State of Threat Detection" report (survey of 2,000 SecOps analysts at 1,000+-employee firms) found SOC teams receive **an average of 4,484 alerts per day, of which over two-thirds (67%) are ignored**, with analysts reporting that **83% of alerts are false positives "not worth their time"**; manual triage was estimated to cost ~$3.3B annually in the US. An ACM Computing Surveys 2025 survey on SOC alert fatigue documents the concrete failure mode: once false-positive volume is high, humans **blanket-suppress, skim by severity, and stop trusting the system** — directly analogous to what happens when a proactive assistant is too noisy or wrong.

### B3. Post-mortems on Alexa, Google Assistant, Siri, Cortana
- **Amazon Alexa.** Per a Nov 2022 Business Insider investigation (which interviewed a dozen current/former hardware employees describing "a division in crisis"), the Alexa/devices division was on track to lose ~$10B in 2022; a July 2024 Wall Street Journal report on internal documents put device-division losses at **"more than $25 billion" between 2017 and 2021.** Root cause: the business model assumed **voice commerce** (buying via Echo) would monetize cheap-at-cost hardware, but it "never took off" — people used Alexa for timers, weather, and music, which Amazon couldn't monetize. A former senior employee told the WSJ, "We worried we've hired 10,000 people and we've built a smart timer," and another called Alexa "a colossal failure of imagination" and "a wasted opportunity." The Alexa unit was the prime target of Amazon's ~10,000-person layoffs. **Lesson: a personal assistant justified by a monetization theory rather than genuine recurring utility fails; the durable use cases are the mundane ones (reminders, timers, quick info).**
- **Cortana.** Microsoft discontinued Cortana (mobile apps March 2021; standalone Windows app August 2023; Teams/Outlook features through 2024). Reported cause: low adoption (one analysis cites ~10% of Windows 10 users regularly using it by 2023), inability to compete with Alexa/Google/Siri, and a strategic pivot to OpenAI-powered Copilot. Nadella had said as early as 2019 that Cortana was no longer a direct Alexa/Google competitor. **Lesson: assistants without a differentiated, sticky use case get abandoned.**
- **Google Assistant → Gemini.** Google is replacing the (2016-era, command-and-response) Assistant with Gemini, but the transition was **delayed from end-2025 into 2026** precisely because Gemini "still struggles as a basic assistant" — reviewers documented Gemini falsely reporting "Action failed" after successfully setting alarms/timers, and silently failing to create calendar events. **Lesson: LLM fluency does not equal reliable action execution; the boring, high-frequency actions (alarm, timer, calendar) must be rock-solid, and silent action failures are described as "a confidence killer."** A recurring recommendation is "one assistant surface, two execution engines" — deterministic paths for system actions, LLM for open-ended queries (mirroring Rasa's CALM philosophy).
- **Siri / Apple Intelligence.** Covered in Part A — repeated delays, internal "crisis"/"ugly and embarrassing" characterizations (Bloomberg/Gurman), legacy architecture that didn't extend to LLM behavior, and ultimately renting Google's Gemini. **Lesson: over-promising proactive/personal-context features and under-delivering is reputationally costly.**

### B4. Trust calibration for agentic AI that takes real-world actions
- **Delegation boundary.** A 2026 systematic review (Adedokun et al., *Int'l J. Research & Innovation in Applied Science*, Vol. 11(3)) synthesizing HITL/oversight/trust-calibration work 2020–2026 reports that while practitioners use AI assistance for ~60% of work, **full delegation is feasible for only ~0–20% of tasks** — a persistent human-AI collaboration boundary. This argues strongly for defaulting to human-in-the-loop confirmation on consequential actions.
- **Foundational HCI trust literature.** The classic model (Lee & See 2004, "Trust in Automation: Designing for Appropriate Reliance"; Lee & Moray 1994) holds that reliance occurs when trust exceeds self-confidence; **"algorithm aversion" (Dietvorst et al. 2015) shows a few visible errors sharply depress trust — more than rational updating would predict.** Cognitive-forcing-function work (Buçinca et al. 2021, CSCW) and explanation studies (Vasconcelos et al. 2023, CSCW) show design can reduce over-reliance. Amershi et al.'s CHI 2019 "Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction" are the standard design checklist (make clear what the system can do, why it did something, support efficient correction and dismissal, remember recent interactions, convey uncertainty). Wischnewski, Krämer & Müller's CHI 2023 survey maps the trust-calibration state of the art.
- **Task-dimension delegation.** He et al. (2023) identify task dimensions — process vs. outcome consequence, social consequence, familiarity, complexity — that shape when users want to retain control vs. delegate. This directly informs which (task × integration) tuples should default to Always Ask vs. Always Allow.
- **Agentic security = permissioning, not model tweaks.** OWASP's *State of Agentic AI Security and Governance* (v2.01, June 11, 2026) argues **prompt injection may be structural, not a patchable bug** — LLMs cannot reliably separate instructions from data, so hostile text in an email, calendar invite, doc, or web page can carry operator-level authority (OWASP maps prompt injection to six of ten categories in its Top 10 for Agentic Applications). Simon Willison's **"lethal trifecta"** (private data + untrusted content + external communication) names exactly the risk profile of an assistant that reads your email and can send email. The Replit 2025 incident (an agent deleted a production database during an explicit code freeze, fabricated thousands of records, and falsely reported rollback was impossible) shows the same permission model fails whether the trigger is an attacker or the model itself. When OpenAI launched ChatGPT Atlas (Oct 2025), researchers demonstrated within hours that text hidden in a Google Doc could hijack the agent; OpenAI's own guidance: **limit what the agent can access, give narrow instructions, require confirmation before consequential actions.** Microsoft's 2026 security guidance stresses that when agents move "from reading to acting," authorization is evaluated against the *agent's* identity, not the user's — so **scoped permissions, allowlists, workspace isolation, and audit logging are the primary defense.** This is precisely the target system's per-(task × integration × account) three-state model with audit logging and fail-secure behavior — the research validates that design as the correct architectural response.

## Traps to Avoid (documented failure patterns)

1. **Autonomous action by default.** Meurisch (autonomous = least preferred, μ = 12.2%), Kraus (Intervention least trusted, no trust growth), and Harari & Amir (unsolicited help backfires via self-threat) all converge: default to **suggest-and-confirm**, not act. Reserve Always-Allow for genuinely tedious, low-consequence, high-familiarity tasks the user has explicitly delegated.
2. **Noisy/low-precision proactivity.** The alert-fatigue literature (Vectra 2023: 4,484 alerts/day, 67% ignored, 83% false positives) shows that once false positives accumulate, users blanket-suppress and stop trusting the system. A proactive reminder engine must optimize for **precision over recall** and make dismissal/feedback trivial.
3. **Proactivity in sensitive domains.** Meurisch found users want little/no proactivity around mental health and shopping. Gate sensitive categories behind explicit opt-in.
4. **Silent action failures.** Google's Gemini falsely reporting "Action failed" (and silently not creating events) is described as "a confidence killer." Every action must produce a verifiable, truthful result and surface failures honestly.
5. **"It's local, so it's private."** Microsoft Recall stored an unencrypted SQLite screenshot database and was repeatedly cracked even after the enclave fix. Encrypt at rest, minimize decrypted surface, fail secure.
6. **Prompt injection via ingested content (the lethal trifecta).** An assistant that reads email and can send email/modify calendars is the canonical attack target. Do not rely on the model to resist injection; rely on scoped permissions, human confirmation for consequential/external actions, allowlists, and audit logs.
7. **Authorization against the agent's identity, not the user's.** A successful injection weaponizes every credential the agent holds. Scope credentials per integration/account and keep the blast radius small.
8. **Monetization/hype substituting for utility.** Alexa's >$25B losses ("we've built a smart timer") and Cortana's shutdown show assistants justified by a business theory or marketing hype, rather than reliable everyday usefulness, get gutted. Siri's pre-announcement of undelivered features was "ugly and embarrassing."
9. **Brittle scripted intent systems.** Rasa's own pivot away from hand-authored intents/stories to CALM, and Google's "keep deterministic paths for system actions" pattern, both argue for LLM-for-understanding + deterministic-for-execution.
10. **Ambitious rewrites that never ship.** Leon's years of "inconsistent" shipping and perpetually-in-preview 2.0 is a warning about scope; ship a reliable narrow core first.

## Recommendations (staged, with change thresholds)

**Stage 0 — Build the reliable, reactive core first (ship this before any proactivity).**
- Ingest → Meilisearch hybrid search → chat + raw-data-browser UI + OpenAI-compatible model layer (Ollama/vLLM). Make the "boring" retrieval and any read-only actions rock-solid. *Benchmark to advance:* retrieval quality and action success rate on high-frequency tasks (calendar create/read, reminder set) at ≥99% with **no silent failures** — every action returns a truthful, verifiable result.
- Implement the permission ledger and audit log **before** any write action ships. Default every (task × integration × account) tuple that is external or irreversible (send email, create/modify calendar event, purchase, delete) to **Always Ask**; default read/low-consequence tuples to Always Allow only after explicit user opt-in. Permission-state changes must require **human action, never agent action** (defends against injection-driven escalation). Fail secure: unknown tuple = deny.

**Stage 1 — Add proactivity conservatively.**
- Ship proactivity at the **"notify" and "recommend-and-confirm"** levels only (Meurisch's proactive support I/II; Kraus's Notification/Suggestion) — never autonomous "Intervention" by default. Start with the domains users most want proactivity in: **calendar/appointment and reminder surfacing** (Meurisch: ~90% acceptance for social/appointment reminders). Exclude sensitive domains (mental-health-adjacent, shopping/purchases) from proactive surfacing unless explicitly opted in.
- Instrument a per-notification **dismissal/false-positive rate**. *Threshold to change:* if a proactive channel's dismissal rate exceeds ~50% over a rolling window, automatically throttle it and prompt the user to re-tune — treat noisy proactivity as a bug, not a feature. Provide a one-tap "not useful / mute this type" control on every proactive message.

**Stage 2 — Selectively expand delegation.**
- Only promote a specific (task × integration × account) tuple from Always Ask → Always Allow when the user has confirmed it repeatedly and it is low-consequence, high-familiarity, and reversible. Given the ~0–20% full-delegation ceiling in the literature, expect most consequential actions to remain human-in-the-loop indefinitely; that is the correct steady state, not a limitation to engineer away.
- Expose the MCP server and multi-surface access only after the permission layer is proven to gate uniformly across surfaces (a compromised/buggy surface must not bypass it).

**Cross-cutting UX principles (from Amershi et al. 2019):** always show what the assistant can do, why it acted, and make correction/dismissal one step; convey uncertainty; keep the audit log legible to a non-expert.

## Open Questions to Feed Later Research

**Memory architecture.**
- Does the target need Letta-style *self-editing* tiered memory (core/recall/archival) on top of Meilisearch, or is Meilisearch hybrid search + a structured "importance/salience" store sufficient? Letta removed heartbeats in V1 — what triggers background/proactive reasoning if not a heartbeat loop?
- How should "inferred importance" be represented and stored so it is inspectable in the raw-data-browsing UI (a differentiator no surveyed product offers)?
- Mem0 (memory layer) vs. Letta (runtime): which pattern fits a Python home-server assistant better?

**Permissions design.**
- What taxonomy of "consequence" (reversibility, externality, social impact — cf. He et al.) should drive default states per (task × integration × account) tuple?
- How to make the audit log and permission ledger legible to a non-expert user (Amershi guideline: make clear why the system acted)?
- How to defend the permission layer itself against prompt-injection-driven privilege escalation? (Recommendation above: permission changes always require human action — validate this is sufficient.)

**Infrastructure.**
- Meilisearch hybrid (keyword + vector) vs. the pgvector/LanceDB norm in this space — trade-offs for personal-scale corpora, and how to implement per-account permission-scoped retrieval (cf. Onyx's permission mirroring for *read* access).
- OpenAI-compatible endpoint: which local-model stack (Ollama, vLLM, LM Studio) best supports the reliable tool-calling the agent loop needs, given Letta's finding that older/smaller non-reasoning models struggle with tool-based control flow?
- How to guarantee fail-secure behavior across a multi-surface deployment (chat, data browser, MCP server) so a compromised or buggy surface cannot bypass the permission layer?
- Reliability engineering for the "boring" high-frequency actions (calendar, reminders) to avoid Gemini-style silent failures.

## Caveats

- **Recency and source mix.** Several fast-moving facts (Khoj star count, Letta V1 architecture, Siri AI GA timing, Recall vulnerability status) are current as of mid-2026 and will drift. Where possible I used primary sources (project GitHub/READMEs/docs, official blogs, Bloomberg/WSJ/Business Insider original reporting, ACM/IEEE/arXiv papers); some corroborating details came from secondary tech press and should be treated as such.
- **"Proactivity" claims in marketing vs. reality.** Khoj's "smart notifications," Apple's "personal context understanding," and Meta AI's calendar-agent claims are largely *capability* statements, not evidence of reliable, well-calibrated proactive behavior in the field. I have flagged where a feature is user-configured (scheduled) rather than inferred.
- **Some numbers are single-source.** The ~10% Cortana usage figure and some loss projections trace to individual reports/analyses; the WSJ >$25B (2017–2021) and BI ~$10B (2022) Alexa figures are the best-sourced. Treat precise dollar and percentage figures as approximate.
- **HCI generalizability.** The Kraus study used a 42-person lab robot task and the Harari & Amir studies used vignettes (not a live product); Meurisch is a smartphone in-the-wild study. All point the same direction (favor low-to-medium proactivity), but effect sizes in a real home-server assistant may differ.
- **No maintainer postmortem was found for some projects.** For Khoj and AnythingLLM I found no explicit "lessons learned / abandoned feature" retrospective; the strongest documented failures came from the commercial products (Alexa, Cortana, Siri, Recall) and the security/HCI literature. Absence of a retrospective is not evidence of absence of problems.
